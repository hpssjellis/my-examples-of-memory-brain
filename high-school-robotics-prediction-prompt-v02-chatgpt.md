# Investigating High School Education, Robotics and Employment in an AI-Driven Economy (1900–2050)

Act as an interdisciplinary research team combining educational economics, labor-market analysis, systems modeling, sociology and technology forecasting.

Investigate how well high school education prepares graduates for skilled, economically sustainable employment, how these relationships have changed over time, and whether accessible, hands-on robotics and mechatronics education could improve future outcomes as AI transforms the economy.

My starting hypothesis is that a grassroots robotics course could benefit a broad range of students, not just those intending to become engineers. Practical experience with electronics, programming, sensors, physical systems, problem-solving and AI tools may provide transferable capabilities for future workers across many occupations.

**Treat this as a hypothesis to investigate, not a conclusion you must prove.** The analysis must be willing to find a small, large, mixed, uncertain or negligible effect.

## 1. Define the outcome

First, propose a defensible definition of successful skilled employment for high school graduates.

Consider:
- Employment within a specified period after graduation, such as one to five years.
- Whether work provides an inflation-adjusted living wage.
- Skilled trades, technical occupations, manufacturing, service occupations, entrepreneurship and other forms of productive employment.
- The changing meaning of skilled work as economies transition from agricultural and industrial employment to services, information technology, automation and AI.

Define the population, denominator, geographic scope and time-to-employment measure. Explain which historical comparisons are defensible and which are not.

Do not assume that the same employment measure can be calculated reliably for every decade from 1900 to 2050.

## 2. Investigate the historical record

Examine the relationship between high school education and employment from 1900 onward, using decade-level analysis where evidence permits.

Investigate relevant developments, including:
- Apprenticeships, family trades and early secondary education.
- Expansion of mass secondary education and industrial employment.
- Postwar economic growth, vocational education and the expansion of higher education.
- Automation, globalization, credential inflation and changes in entry-level work.
- Digital technology, social media and changing patterns of learning and attention.
- Recent generative AI adoption and possible changes to employment opportunities.
- Differences in outcomes by region, socioeconomic background, educational pathway and labor-market conditions.

Do not impose a rising, falling or U-shaped historical curve in advance. Determine what the evidence supports.

Distinguish observed historical data from estimates, interpretations and gaps in the record. Do not fabricate historical percentages to complete a smooth graph.

## 3. Model the wider environment

Consider that schools directly control only part of a student's daily experience. The remainder includes family, sleep, employment, independent learning, social relationships, recreation and digital media.

Investigate whether attention fragmentation, immersive gaming, social media and other forms of digital saturation are associated with educational or employment outcomes. Consider both potential harms and benefits, and distinguish correlation from causation.

Examine whether hands-on, physical, collaborative and project-based learning may strengthen engagement, persistence, problem-solving and practical competence. Identify the mechanisms supported by evidence and those that remain speculative.

Treat the approximate six-hours-at-school and eighteen-hours-elsewhere framework as a simplifying systems hypothesis, not a fixed measurement of effective learning time or a presumption that the non-school environment is harmful.

## 4. Evaluate the grassroots robotics intervention

Model a practical, accessible high school robotics or mechatronics course as a potential intervention.

The course may include:
- Electronics, microcontrollers, sensors and actuators.
- Programming, debugging and iterative physical experimentation.
- Practical problem-solving and systems integration.
- AI-assisted development and an understanding of AI capabilities and limitations.
- Communication, documentation, collaboration and independent learning.
- Projects that can range from simple electronic devices to networked robots and student-designed applications.

Consider whether these experiences could improve employability among students pursuing university, trades, technical college, direct employment or entrepreneurship.

Account for implementation costs, teacher preparation, equipment access, student participation, course quality, employer demand and opportunities for further training.

Compare the intervention with a plausible alternative, such as conventional education, improved general STEM education or other vocational programs. Do not assume robotics is superior to every alternative.

Estimate the likely direction, magnitude, uncertainty and distribution of any employment benefit. Where direct causal evidence is missing, explain what proxy evidence or explicit assumptions would be needed.

## 5. Explore alternative futures to 2050

Develop a transparent scenario model for the period leading to 2050.

Consider multiple plausible futures for:
- AI and autonomous-agent adoption.
- The availability and quality of entry-level work.
- Demand for practical, technical and interpersonal skills.
- Economic growth, inequality and regional labor-market conditions.
- The ability of schools and other educational institutions to adapt.
- The adoption, accessibility and effectiveness of grassroots robotics education.

Include a baseline scenario and one or more intervention scenarios only where the assumptions can be stated clearly.

Allow the trajectories to diverge gradually, remain similar or produce unexpected results. Do not force a split in 2030 or prescribe a final employment rate.

Where useful, include optimistic, central and pessimistic scenarios. Explain which assumptions cause the outcomes to differ and how sensitive the results are to those assumptions.

## 6. Data and visualization

Produce the most defensible historical analysis and future model that the available evidence permits.

Prefer observed data over estimates. If the exact target measure cannot be reconstructed consistently across the full historical period, explain why and use a clearly identified proxy or narrower period where appropriate.

If a numerical scenario model is possible, provide a clean CSV with decade-level values and clearly labeled fields for:
- Decade.
- Observed employment outcome, where available.
- Estimated or modeled baseline outcome.
- Estimated or modeled robotics-intervention outcome, where applicable.
- Evidence status and uncertainty, where practical.

Do not present illustrative assumptions as observed data. Mark unavailable values as missing rather than inventing them. State the units, population, definitions and assumptions.

Generate a line graph if supported. Visually distinguish historical observations, estimates and future scenarios. If the intervention effect is too uncertain to justify a single line, use ranges or scenario bands rather than false precision.

If the evidence does not support a meaningful numerical graph, explain the limitation and propose the most informative alternative visualization.

## 7. Report conclusions and implications

Conclude by addressing:
1. What can be established about historical changes in graduate employment?
2. What evidence supports or challenges the hypothesis that grassroots robotics education could improve employability?
3. Which students, occupations and regions might benefit most or least?
4. What are the plausible scale and limitations of the effect?
5. What alternative interventions might achieve similar or better outcomes?
6. What low-cost, practical experiments could schools conduct to test the hypothesis?
7. What conclusions are sufficiently robust to inform education policy, and what remains uncertain?

Use credible sources, identify major evidence gaps and make assumptions explicit.

The goal is not to prove that traditional education is failing or that robotics is the solution. The goal is to determine whether a practical, accessible robotics course is a defensible investment in the capabilities that graduates may need in an AI-driven economy, and how that claim could be tested.
